Part one

Content

How to train your eye, the formats brands actually buy, and how to film your first three samples this weekend.

Before you make anything, train your eye

Now, this is the one that nobody really talks about, and I genuinely think it's the highest-leverage hour you can spend this week, before you buy a single piece of gear or film a single second of anything.

You cannot cook a dish you have never tasted. And most beginners try to make content before they have ever really watched content, which is why the first videos feel off and nobody can explain why . . .

So before you film a single thing, go and study 10 ads.

How to actually study them
  • Go to the Meta Ad Library and type in a brand you like. It's free, it's public, and it shows you every ad that brand is currently running with real money behind it. TikTok Creative Center does the same thing. Save 10 ads in the category you want to work in.
  • Watch each one three times. Once normally. Once with the sound completely off. And once with your screen face down, listening only.
  • You watch it normally to see whether you'd genuinely keep watching, then on mute, because most people scroll with the sound off and the visuals plus the text on screen have to carry the whole thing alone, and then with the screen face down, which is the honest test of whether the words on their own would sell it.
  • Then write down five things for every ad: the first line they say, the format, roughly how many cuts are in the first 15 seconds, the moment the product first appears, and how they close it.

Do that for 10 ads and you will understand more about how this actually works than most of the people already calling themselves creators, because almost nobody sits down and does it, they just scroll past the same ads a hundred times and never once ask why the ad worked on them . . .

You're doing all of this to learn the grammar of it, the same way you learned how a sentence sounds long before you ever sat down to write one. Copying somebody's video is a dead end, and understanding why theirs worked is a skill that travels with you forever . . .

If you only do one thing from this entire video before the workshop, make it this. Ten ads, three passes each, five notes each. It takes about 40 minutes and it changes what you see forever.

The first three seconds

Basically, brands are buying your hook more than they're buying anything else.

A paid ad lives or dies in the first two seconds, and when a brand is spending real money on distribution, a slow opening is literally money burning in front of them, which is why the single fastest way to look expensive as a creator is just to open well.

Your hook is really three things happening at once, which is what you say, what's on screen behind you, and the text overlay across the top, and all three of them land in the same second and all three have to earn the next one . . .

The opening that kills most beginner videos

"Hey guys, so today I'm going to be talking about . . ."

That's about two and a half seconds of nothing before anything happens. On organic content you might get away with it. On a paid ad, that's the whole budget gone.

Openings that work
  • Start in the middle of the sentence, like the camera caught you halfway through telling a friend.
  • Open on the problem, physically. Show the tangled cable, the sore back, the sink full of dishes.
  • Open on the result and work backwards from it.
  • Open with the product already in your hand and already doing the thing.
  • Open with a real number or a real detail. "I've used the same one of these for five years."

And the practical move that brands genuinely love: film three different hooks for the same video, and hand all three over. It costs you an extra four minutes, it gives them three things to test, and it makes you the creator they come back to when they need volume.

The formats brands actually buy

There are really only a handful of formats that make up almost all paid UGC, and knowing their names is half of it, because a brand brief is going to ask for them by name.

Format 01
Talking head
You, face to camera, telling the story of the product like you are telling a friend. The one everybody starts with.
Format 02
Problem then solution
Show the annoying thing happening first, in real life, then show the fix. The highest converting format in almost every category.
Format 03
Product demo
Hands, close ups, the thing actually working. Very little of you, a lot of the product.
Format 04
Unboxing and first impressions
Package, open, react, first use. Easy to film, and brands run enormous volumes of it.
Format 05
Voiceover b-roll
You never appear. You film footage and narrate over the top. The best starting format if being on camera makes you nervous.
Format 06
Day in the life / POV
The product woven into a real routine. Works beautifully for food, wellness, apps and anything habitual.
Format 07
Green screen reaction
You react to a screenshot, a review, a comment, a post. Cheap to film and endlessly repeatable.
Format 08
Comparison and street style
This one against that one, or asking real people. Higher effort, and brands pay accordingly.

Most creators can only do one of these, usually talking head, because it's the one they've seen a thousand times and it feels like the safest place to start.

If you can do four of them well, you get rebooked, because a brand running ads needs volume and variety more than it needs one perfect video . . .

A note on the ones you'd never guess

Voiceover b-roll is the format almost nobody starts with, and it's the one I'd push you toward if being on camera makes you nervous, because you never show your face at all. You film hands, product and environment, then you narrate over the top of it. Brands buy an enormous amount of this, and it is by far the fastest format to actually get good at . . .

The fundamentals that make content "brand-ready"

Now, none of this is technically difficult. It's just a lot of small things at once, which is exactly why it feels overwhelming at the start. It's like driving. Mirrors, pedals, indicator, all at the same time, and then three weeks later you're doing every bit of it without thinking about it.

Light
Face the window. Natural light on your face beats any ring light you can buy this week.
Sound
Get close, film in a room with soft things in it, and stay away from anywhere with an echo.
Stability
Prop the phone on books or a shelf. A wobbling frame reads as amateur before you say a word.
Framing
Vertical 9:16, eyes in the top third, and leave breathing room top and bottom for the platform's buttons.
Pace
A cut every two to three seconds. Cut out every pause, every um, every breath before a sentence.
Captions
Burn them in. Most of your audience is watching on mute with the phone face down on a desk.
Background
Clean and real. A lived-in kitchen beats a blank white wall every single time.
Delivery
Talk to one friend, not to a room. The second you switch into presenter voice, the whole reason they hired you is gone.
The three that fix the most videos
  • Light. Face the window. That's the whole tip. You do not need a ring light to start, you need to stop filming with the window behind you.
  • Sound. Bad audio kills more videos than bad video does. Get close, film in a room with soft things in it, and stay off the balcony.
  • Cuts. Every time you pause, take a breath or say "um", cut it out. When there's a cut every two or three seconds, a video that felt slow suddenly feels professional.

And the delivery thing that matters more than any of it: talk like you're telling one friend about it, not like you're addressing a room. Brands are paying for content that feels like a real person, so the second you switch into presenter voice, the entire reason they hired you disappears.

How to film your sample videos

This is the part that changes everything for most people, so read this next line twice.

You do not need a brand's permission to make content for them.

Every creator in this industry started out by making videos for products that nobody was paying them to make videos for. It's called spec work, every creative industry on earth does it, and an architect will happily walk you through a building they designed for a pitch they didn't even win . . .

Your first three, this weekend
  • Go around your house and pick three products you already own and genuinely use. Your bathroom cabinet, your kitchen counter, your gym bag, your car, your desk, your toolbox. That's where they are.
  • Pick things you actually use, because you can talk about them for 60 seconds without a script, and that shows on camera in a way you cannot fake.
  • Film each one in a different format. One talking head, one product demo, one voiceover b-roll. Three videos, three formats, three categories, and you're done.
  • Keep each one under 30 seconds, film the hook three ways, and keep the raw footage.
  • Label them honestly on your page as concepts or spec work. Never imply the brand hired you.

That is a weekend of work, and it is the whole difference between a page that says you want to do this and a page that shows a brand exactly what they would be buying from you, which is the only thing they are actually trying to work out . . .

Which brands to go after

Now, this is where most people waste months, so let me save you those months.

Rule one: go after brands that are already running ads

If a brand is running paid ads right now, then they already buy content, they already have a budget line sitting there for it, and creative burnout is already a problem on their desk, which means you're offering them something they were going to buy this month anyway.

If they've never run an ad before, you're doing six weeks of educating them first, and it usually ends at $50 . . .

You can check this in about ten seconds. Meta Ad Library, type the brand name in, and see whether anything is live. Free, public, no login . . .

Rule two: go after the world you already live in

Your job, your hobby, your health thing, your kids, your car, your faith, your sport. Remember, in "U-GC", "you" are the prize. The reason a brand picks you over someone with ten times your following is that you already look and sound like their customer.

Five of our creators. The thing they had before UGC is the thing that got them hired.

Rule three: big enough to have money, small enough to reply

Direct-to-consumer brands, app and software companies, supplements, tools, skincare, home goods, pet and fitness. Companies in that band are big enough to have a real marketing budget sitting there, and small enough that a human being actually reads the inbox, which is the combination you want.

What to walk away from
  • Gifted-only deals where the product is the payment.
  • Anyone paying you in "exposure".
  • Anything asking you to buy the product first and get reimbursed later.
  • The giant legacy brands, at the start. Nike has an agency. They are not hiring you off an email in month one, and chasing them will cost you the months where you could have been building real proof.

Common mistakes going from 0 to 1

Getting the first paid deal is a completely different game from scaling, so here are the ones I watch people make over and over.

The mistake
Waiting until the portfolio is perfect before pitching anybody.
Do this instead
Ship it at 80%. A live page you are still improving beats a perfect page nobody has seen.
The mistake
Sending four pitches, hearing nothing, and deciding it doesn't work.
Do this instead
A 1% reply rate is normal and healthy. Four pitches is not a test, it is a rounding error. Send them daily.
The mistake
Filming content you personally think looks cool.
Do this instead
Film for the brand's customer. Ask what their buyer is worried about, then answer that on camera.
The mistake
No niche, so there is nothing specific to hire you for.
Do this instead
Pick three to five categories that already exist in your real life and write them on the page.
The mistake
Only having one format, usually talking head.
Do this instead
Film your three samples in three different formats so a brand can see range in 90 seconds.

Common mistakes going from 1 to 10

And once you've landed that first one, the game changes completely, because keeping a client and growing them is a totally different set of habits from landing one in the first place . . .

The mistake
Delivering the video and waiting to be asked for more.
Do this instead
Send the next three concepts with the delivery. Most retainers start because the creator proposed one.
The mistake
Charging one flat price and giving everything away inside it.
Do this instead
Usage rights, whitelisting, raw files and extra hooks are all separate line items. Ask for them.
The mistake
Constantly chasing new brands instead of keeping the ones you have.
Do this instead
Keeping one client for a year is a fraction of the work of finding twelve. Repeat clients are the whole game.
The mistake
Never looking at how the content actually performed.
Do this instead
Ask for the numbers. Being able to say the second hook held attention 3x longer makes you the creator they plan around.
The mistake
Putting everything into one brand and one platform.
Do this instead
Diversify on purpose. Three brands paying you $1k is a far safer business than one brand paying you $3k.

The creators who get to ten thousand a month are usually the ones who kept the same client for a year, while everyone else was busy going out and finding a new one every single month.

What your portfolio is actually for

Your portfolio answers three questions, and genuinely that's it:

1
Who are you?
Your name, your face, where you are, and the real life thing that makes you believable on this product.
2
What kind of content do you make?
The niches you create for, the formats you film, and examples a brand can watch in ten seconds.
3
Why should we hire you?
The concrete reasons to give you a first test, and one obvious way to contact you.

If a brand manager can answer all three of those in about 30 seconds, on their phone, standing in line somewhere, then your portfolio works. And if they can't, it genuinely does not matter how pretty it is . . .

So every single decision on that page comes back to those three questions . . . If something on that page answers one of the three, it earns its place. Everything else is decoration.

What all six have in common

Now, same drill as the content. Don't just look at these and feel behind. Study them.

Open all six in tabs and hunt for what's the same across all of them, because whatever is the same is the actual rule, and whatever is different is just personal taste.

This is what I find every single time I do it, and the counts are from actually going through all six of them side by side:

6of 6
Name, one line of positioning and contact details, right at the top. The name is always the biggest thing on the page.
6of 6
The work is grouped by category, never by date. Category header, row of cards, category header, row of cards.
6of 6
Vertical 9:16 cards with a caption underneath every single one. The most repeated unit in the entire category.
6of 6
A plain text email address at the bottom, selectable, not hidden behind a form.
5of 6
The niches written out as visible labels or chips.
4of 6
Photography treated as its own section, separate from video.
4of 6
An About Me block built on real life credibility.

A nav bar. So you do not need one.

A single client case study in depth.

So the rules are pretty clear: name huge at the top, one line saying what you do, contact visible immediately, work grouped by category, vertical cards with a caption under each one, and a plain text email at the bottom . . .

And the things you can happily skip: rates, a nav bar, testimonials, a logo wall. Most of these pages don't have them, and the ones that do aren't better for it.

So your job, your background, your age, the thing you've done for nineteen years, the thing your friends already ask you about, all of that goes in the first line of your page . . . That's the highest-leverage sentence you will write.

The sections your page needs, in order

This is the running order. Nine sections, and every one of them is fillable today, with no past clients.

The hero
Your name set large, one line on what you create and who for, where you are based, and your email. Put your real life credential in that first line. HVAC technician, 19 years. Aesthetic nurse. Mum of three. Native Spanish speaker.
Your niches
Three to five categories, written out plainly. This is how a brand decides in two seconds whether you are relevant to them.
Formats you film
Straight from the list earlier. Talking head, problem solution, demo, voiceover b-roll. It tells a brand exactly what they can brief you on.
Your samples
Your spec videos as 9:16 cards, with a caption under each one naming the product and the format. Mark them clearly as concepts.
Why brands should test you
Four to six short factual bullets about attributes rather than results. Real gym at home. 24 hour turnaround. Comfortable on camera. Speaks two languages.
What I bring to a shoot
Your environment, your gear, your turnaround time. A brand paying for a location shoot is very interested in the fact you already have a workshop, a nursery, a truck or a commercial kitchen.
Photos
Real photos of you, your space, your setup. This is the one section a beginner can always fill honestly, and four of the six swipes treat photography as its own deliverable anyway.
Products you actually use
The brands already in your house. It shows category fit and it doubles as your pitch list.
Contact
Your email in plain text and a link to book a call. Nothing clever. Make it impossible to miss.

If you don't have video yet, photos do the job. Real photos of you, your space, your setup, your work. A page with five honest photos reads far better than a page with an empty section on it.

The rules you cannot break

Every section has to be full, and every section has to be true. There are genuinely no exceptions to this one, because breaking it is the single fastest way to destroy a page that was otherwise working . . .

The nevers
  • Never write "past clients: none", and never leave an empty section sitting on the page.
  • Never place a greyed out logo of a brand you have not worked with.
  • Never invent a testimonial, a view count, a ROAS figure, a rate or a brand relationship.
  • Never label a concept in a way a brand could mistake for delivered work. Write the word concept on the card.
  • Never apologize on your own page. No "new creator", no "just starting out", no "looking for my first opportunity".
  • Never ship a section you cannot fill honestly. Cut it instead, and the page gets stronger.

And the reason for all of it gets pretty simple once you think about who is actually reading the page, and what they are trying to work out in the thirty seconds they give you.

A brand manager looking at a beginner already knows you haven't performed for other brands yet, and that's exactly why the first test is $150 and not $5,000.

What they're actually deciding is whether you're worth that first test. And it comes down to six things, all six of which you already have today:

1
Face and delivery
Can you hold a camera and talk like a human being.
2
Environment
What rooms, kitchens, gyms, cars, gardens and worksites do you already have access to.
3
Category fit
What world do you already live in, and does their product live there too.
4
Credibility
Is there a real life reason to believe you on this specific topic.
5
Ideas
Can you come up with an angle, or do they have to spoon feed you every shot.
6
Reliability
Will you hit the deadline and actually follow the brief.

Not one of those six things requires a single past client, which is exactly why a beginner page can be completely full, completely honest, and still get you hired on a first test.

Then you add one sample a week, and in a month you've got four of them, and in three months you've got twelve, and at that point you have a real portfolio and you'll barely remember being nervous about filming the first one . . .
